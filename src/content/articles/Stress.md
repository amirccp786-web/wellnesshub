Health issues are linked to excessive stress. Can stress levels be quantified precisely? 

Although stress is a normal and unavoidable aspect of life, many people believe they are under too much stress. However, "excessive stress" cannot be defined objectively. Many people find stress difficult to describe or measure. 

Stress can be measured in several ways. These examine biomarkers, or physiological reactions, to assess how your body reacts to stress.

## **Stress** 

### **How is Stress Measured?** 

Stress consists of two parts: 

- Stress triggers: the things that cause stress. 

- Stress response: your emotional, physiological, or mental reactions to stressors. 

Determining the triggers or reactions of stress is a common step in the assessment process. One common approach is to evaluate major life changes. However, stress tolerance varies widely: a situation that causes extreme stress for one person may be easily manageable for another. 

The following methods measure your body’s physiological response to stress. By monitoring biomarkers such as heart rate and brainwaves, these techniques evaluate the direct physical impact of stress on your system. 

### **Heart Rate Variability (HRV)** 

Stress is frequently measured via heart rate variability (HRV) analysis. Record the time difference between successive heartbeats. To put it another way, it examines changes in the time between heartbeats and the rate at which your heart is beating. 

Your autonomic nervous system (ANS) regulates HRV. The sympathetic nervous system, which controls the fight-or-flight response, and the parasympathetic nervous system, which controls relaxation, are both parts of the autonomic nervous system (ANS). 

Your ANS is unbalanced when you are constantly in fight-or-flight mode. Your HRV may reflect this imbalance. HRV is higher when you're calm and lower when you're in fight-or-flight mode. Improved cardiovascular health and stress resilience are linked to high HRV. 

A medical expert can use an electrocardiogram to assess your HRV. HRV can also be measured by personal wearables such as chest strap monitors. 

### **Waves in the Brain** 

Electroencephalograms (EEGs) record brainwaves, which research shows can reliably indicate stress levels. 

A 2020 study found that alpha asymmetry—an imbalance in alpha brainwave activity between the brain’s left and right sides—may serve as a key biomarker for stress. 

Mental health practitioners employ EEG-based neurofeedback to evaluate brainwave activity in real time and offer positive reinforcement in order to achieve specific treatment goals.

### **Testing for Hormones** 

Adrenaline and cortisol are two hormones linked to stress. 

When you're under stress, your body produces energy and releases adrenaline to deal with the stressor. It's a component of the fight-or-flight reaction, which explains why anxiety can make you restless. 

At the same time, the adrenal glands release cortisol, another crucial hormone that sustains this emergency response. 

Cortisol plays a role in controlling: 

- Blood sugar 

- Inflammation 

- The metabolism 

Your cortisol levels often change during the day. While cortisol and adrenaline are neither "bad," persistently high cortisol levels can be detrimental to your health. For instance, it could lead to the following: 

- Acne 

- Having trouble focusing 

- Exhaustion 

- A headache 

- Elevated blood pressure 

- Anger 

- Mood issues 

- Weakening in the muscles 

- Weight gain 

Lab testing can use blood or urine samples to measure cortisol levels. You can buy at-home cortisol testing kits, which typically test urine to measure cortisol. 

### **The PSS, or Perceived Stress Scale** 

In 1983, a questionnaire called the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) was created. It assesses your perceived stress level. 

In contrast to the aforementioned stress measurement techniques, this tool relies on your subjective assessment of stress. Instead of focusing on the events you are currently experiencing, the questions focus on your emotional and mental condition. 

Using the PSS to check in with oneself might be beneficial. It is accessible in PDF format. 

### **Stress Trackers: what are they?** 

Many fitness trackers and smartwatches claim to use heart rate and heart rate variability (HRV) to quantify stress. However, due to a dearth of solid study, their dependability is still in doubt. More importantly, because these wearables rely almost entirely on heart metrics, they offer a narrow view of a stress response that actually involves your whole body. 

### **What Stress Levels are Considered "Normal"?** 

While occasional stress is a normal part of life, chronic stress can take a serious toll on your health. Since everyone's experience of stress is unique, there is no set cutoff point at which it becomes "too much." 

However, if you are experiencing physical symptoms, struggle to unwind, or consistently feel overwhelmed and unable to cope, it may be time to consult a doctor or therapist. 

### **Signs of Unhealthy Stress Levels** 

Different people may have different symptoms from high levels of stress. Among the symptoms are: 

- Acne 

- Fear 

- Persistent ache 

- Depression 

- Trouble falling asleep 

- Digestive problems 

- Exhaustion 

- Recurrent sickness 

- Headaches 

- Anger 

- Pain in the abdomen 

- Gaining weight 

Even though other factors can cause physical or emotional symptoms, consult a doctor or therapist if you suspect stress is the cause.

### **Stress Management Advice** 

Stress can be effectively managed in several simple ways: 

- **Try Exercising:** Choose a physical activity you truly enjoy. While any form of exercise helps, yoga is particularly effective at reducing stress. 

- **Engage in Deep Breathing:** By stimulating your parasympathetic neural system, deep breathing can promote relaxation, according to a 2018 study. 

- **Limit Screen Time:** Research from 2018 suggests that excessive screen time may have a detrimental effect on your mental and emotional health. Make an effort to take breaks from your screens during the day. 

- **Spend Time with Others:** According to 2020 research, spending time with others helps to reduce feelings of stress and loneliness. If family is not nearby, attend seminars, religious services, or meetup groups to get regular human interaction. 

- **Practice Mindfulness:** Daily meditation is a proven way to relieve stress and support overall well-being. If you're new to the practice, guided meditation apps or videos are a great place to start. 

- **Spend Time in Nature:** According to 2020 research, spending time in nature can lower stress and boost your emotional state. Pick up an outdoor sport, take lunch outside, or take a walk in a nearby park or natural area. 

- **Seek Targeted Support:** You don't have to navigate difficult life challenges alone. Joining a specialized support group—such as a grief group after losing a loved one—can help you process complex emotions alongside people who understand. 

Speaking with a therapist could be beneficial if you experience frequent stress. Good therapy may help anyone develop stress resistance and deal with stressful situations in a safe setting. If you are worried about the cost of therapy, think about other less expensive possibilities.

### **Let's Review** 

While stress is a normal part of daily life, tracking it—whether through heart rate variability analysis or hormone testing—isn't a requirement for seeking help. You don't need data to justify taking care of yourself. If managing stress could improve your well-being, consider reaching out to a therapist or adopting effective stress-reduction strategies today.
